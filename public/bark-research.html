Procedural bark as real geometry

Research note — why my two attempts failed by construction, what the literature says bark actually is, and the architecture that produces one separate mesh per piece of bark. PlantGenerator, background document.

1. What I got wrong, precisely

The error

Both attempts represented bark as a height field: a single continuous surface whose vertices were pushed along their normals. Attempt 1 displaced the trunk skin; attempt 2 displaced a flat grid. The fracture simulation underneath was reasonable — the failure was in the primitive, and no amount of parameter tuning could fix it.

A displaced surface cannot express any of the four features that define plated bark:

Feature of real barkHeight fieldWhy
Separate piecesimpossibleOne connected surface. Two "scales" are always joined by the material in the groove between them.
A gap you can see intoimpossibleA groove has a floor. A real fissure is a void between two solids that reaches down to the layer beneath.
Thickness / an exposed edgeimpossibleA surface has no thickness. A scale is a slab with a visible broken edge several millimetres thick.
Lifted, curling, shedding edgesimpossibleOverhangs are not functions of the base surface — a height field is single-valued by definition.

So the honest summary of the last two rounds: the pattern generator was worth keeping, the geometry stage was categorically wrong. Bark is a thick shell that has been broken into pieces, and it has to be modelled as solids, not as relief.

2. What bark is, physically

The outer bark (rhytidome) is dead, rigid, and of substantial thickness. The cambium keeps adding wood, the stem's girth grows, and the rhytidome cannot grow with it — it goes into tangential tension and fractures through its full thickness. New periderms form underneath, so the rhytidome is a stack of layers; scales detach along those planes and expose fresher tissue below, which is what the pale patches in the reference photographs are [6][7].

Numbers worth designing against

Relative bark thickness
3.5–20 % of stem diameter for spruce/beech/oak; up to 27 % for oak [8]. 15–26 % of stem radius across savanna/pocosin species [9]. Pine bark 17–20 % of stem volume [10].
Thickness vs. diameter
Grows sub-linearly: allometric exponent ≈ 0.74 (range 0.38–1.20) — big stems invest relatively less [9].
Fragment size
Cracking saturates when cells reach a size proportional to the layer thickness; past that, more stress widens existing cracks instead of making new ones [2].
Curvature effect
On curved substrates, larger curvature radius → smaller fragments and more edges per fragment (scaling law from peridynamic simulation + experiment) [13].

The consequence for modelling: bark thickness is not a decorative parameter. It sets the plate size, and it is 5–20 % of the stem radius — on a 30 cm-radius trunk that is 1.5–6 cm of real, visible slab edge.

3. The literature, and which parts are usable

3.1 Bi-layer fracture simulation — the pattern

Federl & Prusinkiewicz model bark as a rigid layer bonded to a growing substrate, discretised first as a mass-spring lattice and later with wedge finite elements on a growing surface [3][4][5]. Dale, Runions, Hobill & Prusinkiewicz use the same formulation for grasstree bark [11]. These produce crack networks with the right statistics; they do not, by themselves, produce per-piece geometry.

3.2 Hierarchical crack networks — the statistics to hit

Cracks form sequentially: a new crack nucleates in the largest remaining domain and curves to meet an older crack at a right angle, because that maximises its energy release rate. The signature is T-junctions, curved edges, and a narrow cell-size distribution [1][2][12]. Y-junction (120°) networks are a different, more mature regime produced by repeated crack/heal cycles — mud in Death Valley, columnar basalt — not fresh bark [2].

Directly relevant benchmark

An algorithms paper compares random homogeneous tessellation, recursive Voronoi and a crack-growth simulation against image analysis of real hierarchical crack patterns [14]. Verdict: tessellations inherently fail — "the cracks are strictly linear, whereas in real-world systems they are curved"; "an absence of T-junctions"; "the cells vary greatly in size, which is not typical". Only the crack-growth simulation reproduces the real angle, size, side-count and circularity distributions.

So: no Voronoi shattering. That rules out the standard VFX approach for this material, even though it is the easy one.

3.3 Lefebvre & Neyret, Synthesizing Bark — the closest prior art

The one paper that targets exactly this problem and outputs geometry [15]. Their model:

  • bark is represented as circular strips around the trunk, transverse to the growth direction;
  • each strip alternates quasi-rigid epidermal elements with soft fracture elements. When the bark breaks, a new fracture element is inserted between the tear edges, splitting the epidermal element in two;
  • subsequent growth widens those fracture elements — the tear opens as the tree grows;
  • fractures propagate from strip to strip in world space, so they cross branch junctions correctly;
  • finally the fracture silhouettes are reconstructed and meshed (constrained Delaunay), with the tear interiors as their own surfaces.

The important idea I did not use: the fissure is an explicit element with its own width and its own geometry, not the low part of a displacement curve. Their epidermal elements are the ancestors of "pieces". Their reported limitation is that everything is driven by 1D strips, which suits furrowed bark better than tessellated plates.

3.4 Film–substrate fracture with debonding — why plates lift and shed

The modern mechanics literature models exactly the configuration we have — a stiff film on an expanding curved substrate — with interfacial cohesive peridynamics, and crucially couples film cracking with interfacial debonding [13][16][17][18]. Findings that matter here:

  • three fragmentation regimes: cracking with perfect bonding; cracking with minor interfacial damage; debonding-dominated — which is the regime where plates lift and shed [17];
  • fragment size and edge count scale with substrate curvature radius [13];
  • the same framework reproduces crocodile, elephant and rhino skin patterning [18] — the shared mechanism with bark is not a coincidence.

I do not propose running peridynamics. I propose taking its state variables: per-piece bond state (bonded / partially debonded / shed), because that is the switch that turns a flat mosaic into bark with lifted, curling, missing scales.

3.5 Fracture-to-mesh practice

Voronoi cell fracture is normally implemented as clipping a convex solid against the bisector half-space of every rival seed; each shard stays closed, and the useful part is the validation discipline: every shard closed (each edge used by exactly two faces), piece volumes summing to the original, no piece claimed by the wrong seed [19]. Concave input needs a real CSG kernel. We can avoid general CSG entirely — see below.

4. Proposed architecture

Five stages. The output is the trunk skin as one manifold mesh, plus N separate closed solids, one per piece of bark.

Stage 1 — build the bark shell

Offset the existing trunk skin outward by a thickness t(z) taken from the allometry in §2 (5–20 % of local radius, sub-linear in diameter). This gives an inner surface (the substrate, where bark meets living tissue) and an outer surface. The shell between them is the material that will be broken. No CSG needed: both surfaces share the trunk's own quad parameterisation.

Stage 2 — the crack network

Crack-growth simulation in the unrolled (θ, arc-length) domain, as in §3.2: pick the largest remaining domain, start a crack on its boundary, meander across it, turn to terminate perpendicular on an older crack, split, repeat until cells reach the size set by shell thickness. Keep the vector network — a planar graph of curved edges — not a raster. Anisotropy of the domain-splitting rule gives species character (elongated pine plates vs. isotropic plane-tree scales).

while (domain = largest cell with area > k · t²):
    p0   = point on domain boundary, biased across its long axis
    path = grow(p0, meander, steer_towards_perpendicular_on_approach)
    if path ends on an existing crack and both halves are substantial:
        split domain into two cells, record the crack polyline

Stage 3 — one solid per piece the part that was missing

For each cell of the network:

  1. Inset the cell polygon by half the fissure width. The inset is what creates a real gap between neighbouring pieces — the fissure is a void, not a groove.
  2. Lift the inset polygon to the outer surface and project a copy to the inner surface.
  3. Loft between them to make side walls. Give the walls a slight inward taper (pieces are wedges, wider at the top on old bark) and round the top edge — a broken bark edge is eroded, not a knife edge.
  4. Quad-mesh the top face by clipping a regular grid to the polygon and stitching a boundary ring, so the piece stays all-quad and matches the rest of the project's topology rules.
  5. Result: a closed, manifold, all-quad solid. Separate object. Exportable on its own.

Stage 4 — bond state: bonded, lifted, shed

From the debonding literature (§3.4), give each piece an interfacial state:

StateGeometryReads as
bondedsits flush on the inner surfacetight plate
partially debondedone edge rotated outward about the opposite edge; the gap under it is reala scale about to fall off — the overhang a height field cannot do
shedpiece deleted; the layer beneath is exposedthe pale scars in the reference photos

Stage 5 — layered rhytidome

Repeat stages 1–4 for a second (and optionally third) shell underneath, each with its own independent crack network and its own thickness. Shedding a piece in the outer layer reveals intact pieces of the layer below — which is why real exfoliating bark reads as depth rather than as a pattern.

6. Cost, and what is genuinely hard

Budget

A 6 m trunk, 0.6 m diameter, 8 cm plates ≈ 1 500–3 000 pieces. At ~200 quads each (top grid + walls) that is 300–600 k quads for the trunk's bark — comparable to what the tree already handles, and each piece is independent, so LOD is trivial: distant trunks get fewer, larger pieces or fall back to the plain skin.

Hard parts, honestly

Inset of a concave polygon self-intersects on thin necks — needs a straight-skeleton or a clipped-offset with collapse handling. Curvature: pieces are built in the unrolled domain and mapped back, so a piece spanning a strongly curved region must be re-fitted or subdivided. Branch junctions: the unrolled parameterisation breaks down at forks — Lefebvre & Neyret solved propagation there by working in world space [15], and that is the approach to copy.

7. What I am asking before writing code

Three decisions change the architecture, and I would rather have them from you than guess a fourth time:

  1. Pieces as separate objects, or welded into one mesh? Separate is what you asked for and is better for shedding/simulation; a single merged mesh is easier to export as one object. Both are producible — it is one flag.
  2. Which bark type first? The two photographs are different families: irregular polygonal scales (plane/pine plates) vs. vertically elongated blocky plates. Same machinery, different anisotropy and shed rate.
  3. Flat test area again, or straight onto the trunk? A flat patch iterates in seconds and isolates the piece geometry from the curvature problems; the trunk is the real target.
